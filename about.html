The 41 extant Felidae species exhibit the greatest diversity in fur patterns of all terrestrial carnivores. Cats have retractile claws, slender muscular bodies and strong flexible forelimbs. Their teeth and facial muscles allow for a powerful bite. They are all obligate carnivores, and most are solitary predators ambushing or stalking their prey. Wild cats occur in Africa, Europe, Asia, and the Americas. Some wild cat species are adapted to forest and savanna habitats, some to arid environments, and a few also to wetlands and mountainous terrain. Their activity patterns range from nocturnal and crepuscular to diurnal, depending on their preferred prey species.

Early classifications divided Felidae into three subfamilies: Pantherinae, Felinae, and Acinonychinae. Modern molecular studies revised this, merging Acinonychinae into Felinae. Today, Felidae is divided into Pantherinae (big cats like lions and tigers) and Felinae (smaller cats), based on genetic and anatomical differences.

The first cats emerged during the Oligocene period about 25 million years ago, evolving from early ancestors such as Proailurus and Pseudaelurus. These ancient species gave rise to modern felids as well as extinct saber-toothed cats in the Machairodontinae subfamily.

Other “false saber-toothed cats,” such as Barbourofelidae and Nimravidae, were not true felids but close relatives. Together with Felidae, they belong to the broader Feliformia group, which also includes hyenas, mongooses, and related carnivores.
